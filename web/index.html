Concept & build plan · Oct 2026

A bracelet that finds your friends when your phone is dead.

Firefly is a hinged gold bangle with a bezel-set opalite stone that glows, two gold bead buttons and its own radio hidden inside. It keeps a friend group connected inside a packed bar or club with no signal, it never needs the wearer's phone, and when you get home it lets your people know. One friend with battery runs the app; everyone else just wears the band.

Quick answers

Battery, weight, network, cost

Battery

~26 h

On a 110 mAh cell hidden in the clasp, at a 4.1 mA average: listening for the group, a beacon every second, a few alerts and a few minutes of finding. Heavy finding all night brings it to about 16 h, still inside the 12–24 h target. Charges in about 1.5 h on a magnetic cable.

Weight

~24 g

About 25 g for the whole bangle (hollow gold-plated band, centerpiece, clasp box and electronics), against the 30 g limit. That's a typical statement bangle; most smartwatches weigh 30–50 g.

Network

LoRa 915 MHz
+ Bluetooth LE

Bands talk to each other directly over LoRa radio: no phone, Wi-Fi or cell signal needed, reaching 300 m+ indoors. Bluetooth handles close-range finding (0–25 m) and the link to the friend whose phone runs the app.

Cost

~$76

Per prototype band in off-the-shelf parts (itemized below). About $21 at 1,000 units with a custom board and aluminum shell, which supports a $69–89 retail price.

Why

What goes wrong on a night out

Phones die

Photos, Maps, location sharing and group chats drain the battery before the night ends. The person with the dead phone is the one nobody can reach.

No signal, no sightline

Basements and packed floors kill GPS and cell reception. Even with a shared location you cannot spot someone across a dark, loud room.

Groups split

Drinks, restrooms, Ubers. One person ends up coordinating everyone, and someone gets left behind without anyone noticing.

Requirements the design has to meet

  • Under 30 g, looks like jewelry (not a tracker), no screen
  • Lets your people know when you get home
  • 12–24 h battery, works with the wearer's phone dead
  • Splash-proof (spilled drinks, rain), survives crowd impacts
  • Finds connected friends within range; guides by vibration and light
  • Distinguishes "come find me" from "I need help"
  • One or two simple actions, usable in the dark
  • Private by default, affordable

How the two roles work

Every friend wears a band. The responsible friend, whoever still has phone battery, runs the Firefly app. Their phone pairs with their own band over Bluetooth and becomes the group's bridge: bands talk to each other directly over a 915 MHz long-range radio, and the app turns those signals into a live group view, a map, and alerts.

The non-responsible friend needs nothing but the band. Finding, "come find me", check-ins and SOS all run band-to-band without any phone.

    Why these radios

    Bluetooth LE (built into the nRF52840) gives short-range proximity between bands and the link to the responsible friend's phone. Signal strength falls off predictably over 0–25 m indoors, which is what drives the hot/cold vibration in finding mode.

    LoRa at 915 MHz (SX1262) is the phone-independent backbone. It is license-free in the US, punches through walls and crowds far better than 2.4 GHz, and reaches hundreds of metres indoors and over a kilometre outdoors at a few bytes per message, which is all a check-in or an SOS needs. Meshtastic devices use the same chip.

    Not included on purpose: GPS (indoor accuracy is poor and it costs 25 mA), Wi-Fi, and a screen. A future version could add ultra-wideband for true arrow-style direction.

    Build guide · prototype

    How to actually build one

    Everything below is for the off-the-shelf prototype: the two Seeed boards, a battery, a motor, four LEDs, two gold bead buttons, an opalite cabochon, a gold-plated printed centerpiece and clasp box, and a hinged gold-plated bangle. Budget about $76 in parts per band, an afternoon for the first one, and under three hours for each one after that. Build at least two so you can test band-to-band.

    Tools

    • Temperature-controlled soldering iron with a fine tip, leaded or lead-free solder, flux pen
    • Tweezers, wire strippers for 30 AWG, flush cutters
    • Multimeter (continuity and current ranges)
    • 3D printer (resin gives the nicest finish; 0.2 mm FDM works) or a print service like Craftcloud / JLC3DP

    Safety

    • LiPo cells: never puncture, never solder directly to the cell tabs if you can avoid it, charge only through the XIAO's charger, keep the protection board.
    • 915 MHz is a license-free ISM band in the US for hobby use; selling a product needs FCC Part 15 testing. In the EU use 868 MHz.
    • Keep the antenna connected whenever the radio transmits; transmitting into nothing can damage the SX1262.

    Wiring: which pin goes where

    The Wio-SX1262 takes eight of the XIAO's eleven pins through the stacked header, so the buttons share one analog pin with a resistor ladder, the LEDs use one data pin, and the motor uses one. Pin names are the XIAO's silkscreen labels.

    Assembly, step by step

    1. Print and plate the centerpiece and clasp box. The centerpiece is a 28 × 24 × 11 mm pillowed cushion (7.5 mm corners, 3.5 mm rounded edges, 1.0 mm walls) with a 1.9 mm dome, a raised oval collet for the 15 × 11 mm stone, a ring of 0.6 mm milgrain beads, a gadrooned gallery around the waist and two seats for the bead buttons. A 6 × 3.2 mm channel runs under it for the bangle. The clasp box is 35 × 14 × 8 mm. Print in resin at 0.05 mm, sand to 1500 grit, apply conductive paint, and electroplate copper then 18k gold (or send them to a plating service). STL files: hardware/firefly_centerpiece_base.stl, firefly_centerpiece_dome.stl and firefly_clasp_box.stl in the GitHub repo.
    2. Flash the XIAO first. Plug it in over USB-C, install the Seeed nRF52 board package (steps below), and upload the Blink example. If the orange LED blinks, the board is good. Do this before soldering anything; a dead board is easier to return bare.
    3. Stack the radio. Solder low-profile headers into the Wio-SX1262 so the XIAO sits on top of it, USB-C up, with about 1.5 mm between the boards. Trim the pins flush underneath. Plug the FPC antenna into the U.FL socket; it clicks. Route the antenna out the back edge of the stack.
    4. Build the button ladder. Glue two 3 × 3 × 1.5 mm tactile switches on top of the XIAO's shield (insulated with Kapton) under the two bead seats, 21 mm apart, and glue a Ø3.8 mm gold bead onto each plunger through the dome; Button 2's bead has a tiny set stone. Wire: 3V3 → 10 kΩ → D0; D0 → Button 1 → 3.3 kΩ → GND; D0 → Button 2 → 15 kΩ → GND. Check with the multimeter: D0 should read 3.3 V idle, about 0.8 V with Button 1 held, about 2.0 V with Button 2.
    5. Wire the motor driver. MOSFET source to GND, gate to D6 through 100 Ω with 10 kΩ to GND, drain to one motor lead; other motor lead to 3V3. Diode across the motor leads, stripe toward 3V3. 47 µF across 3V3 and GND right at the motor. The motor lives in the clasp box: glue it on top of the battery so it presses against the skin.
    6. Make the LED strip. Four SK6812 Mini-E on a 13 × 3 mm strip, 3.5 mm apart, so they sit right under the stone, DOUT of each to DIN of the next. 330 Ω from D7 to the first DIN. VDD to 3V3, GND to GND. Test with the NeoPixel "strandtest" example before you build it in.
    7. Battery and charging. The battery and charge pins live in the clasp box. Run two 34 AWG enamelled wires from BAT+ and BAT− on the XIAO through the hollow bangle (and past the hinge with a small slack loop) to the battery's JST plug, and two more from the 5V/GND pads to the charge pins. Check polarity twice: red is +. Wrap the cell in Kapton. The XIAO's charge LED should light when the magnetic cable is attached.
    8. Dry fit. Board stack in the centerpiece with the LED strip under the stone and the antenna curved along the inside back wall; battery and motor in the clasp box. Nothing should need force. If the board stack is more than 8 mm tall, shorten the header pins.
    9. Flash the real firmware and test the radios. Upload the Firefly sketch (below) to two bands. With both on the bench, a double-press on one should buzz the other within a second. Walk one band down the hall; it should still receive at the far end of the building. Then test finding mode: the pulse should speed up as the bands approach.
    10. Measure current. Put the multimeter in series with the battery. Idle should sit under 5 mA average with the LoRa listen window on, and under 0.1 mA with the radio off. If it is higher, the SX1262 is not sleeping between windows; check radio.sleep() in the loop.
    11. Set the stone and close up. Drop the opalite into the collet and burnish the bezel over it with a bezel pusher, seal around the bead buttons with a thin ring of silicone so they still click, seat the gasket and snap the dome onto the base around the bangle. Snap the clasp box closed. Test the seal with a damp paper towel before trusting it near drinks.
    12. Fit the bangle. Open the hinged bangle, slide the centerpiece onto the top of the band and the clasp box onto the bottom, and glue both in place with E6000 once the wires are routed. Size: a 62 × 48 mm inside oval fits most 155–175 mm wrists.
    13. Pair a group. Hold Button 1 on every band for 5 seconds within 10 seconds of each other; the bands exchange a random group key over LoRa and buzz twice when done. The app pairs to its wearer's band over Bluetooth from the Group tab.

    Firmware setup

    1. Install Arduino IDE 2.x.
    2. File → Preferences → Additional boards URL: https://files.seeedstudio.com/arduino/package_seeeduino_boards_index.json
    3. Boards Manager → install Seeed nRF52 Boards. Select Seeed XIAO nRF52840.
    4. Library Manager → install RadioLib (SX1262 driver) and Adafruit NeoPixel (SK6812). Bluetooth comes with the board package (Bluefruit).
    5. Open the sketch, set GROUP_SEED to any number, and upload. Double-tap the XIAO's reset button if the port does not appear.

    Shortcut for testing the radio: flash Meshtastic on two stacks first. It supports this exact XIAO + Wio-SX1262 pairing, and proves the hardware before you debug your own code.

    Firmware skeleton

    #include <RadioLib.h>
#include <Adafruit_NeoPixel.h>
#include <bluefruit.h>

SX1262 radio = new Module(D4, D1, D2, D3);   // NSS, DIO1, RESET, BUSY
Adafruit_NeoPixel leds(4, D7, NEO_GRB + NEO_KHZ800);
const int PIN_BTN = A0, PIN_MOTOR = D6, PIN_RFSW = D5;
enum Msg : uint8_t { CHECKIN=1, COME_FIND, HELP, SOS, CANCEL };
struct __attribute__((packed)) Packet {
  uint8_t magic = 0xF1, type, from; uint16_t seq; uint32_t mac; };
uint8_t myId = 1;  uint16_t seq = 0;  uint32_t groupKey;   // set at pairing

void setup() {
  pinMode(PIN_MOTOR, OUTPUT);  pinMode(PIN_RFSW, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(PIN_RFSW, HIGH);                 // antenna switch on
  leds.begin();  leds.setBrightness(100);
  radio.begin(915.0, 125.0, 9, 7, 0x12, 20);    // MHz, kHz, SF9, CR4/7, sync, dBm
  radio.setDio2AsRfSwitch(true);  radio.setTCXO(1.6);
  radio.startReceive();
  Bluefruit.begin();  startAdvertising();       // group hash + myId, 1 s
}
int buttons() {                                 // resistor ladder on A0
  int v = analogRead(PIN_BTN);                  // 12-bit, 3.3 V reference
  return v > 3600 ? 0 : v > 2200 ? 2 : v > 950 ? 1 : 3; }
void buzz(int ms) { digitalWrite(PIN_MOTOR, HIGH); delay(ms); digitalWrite(PIN_MOTOR, LOW); }
void send(uint8_t type) {
  Packet p; p.type = type; p.from = myId; p.seq = ++seq; p.mac = hmac32(p, groupKey);
  radio.transmit((uint8_t*)&p, sizeof p);  radio.startReceive(); }
void loop() {
  switch (gesture(buttons())) {                 // single / double / long, per button
    case B1_SINGLE: findingMode(); break;        // BLE scan → RSSI → pulse period
    case B1_DOUBLE: send(COME_FIND); break;
    case B1_LONG:   send(CHECKIN); buzz(80); break;
    case B2_SINGLE: send(HELP); buzz(150); break;
    case B2_DOUBLE: send(CANCEL); break;
    case B2_LONG:   send(SOS); sosPattern(); break;
  }
  Packet p;
  if (radio.available() && radio.readData((uint8_t*)&p, sizeof p) == RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE
      && p.magic == 0xF1 && p.mac == hmac32(p, groupKey)) handle(p);
  radio.startReceive();
  sd_app_evt_wait();                            // sleep until a BLE/LoRa/timer event
}

    gesture() is the same single/double/long timing the simulation uses (320 ms double-press window, 0.9 s and 3 s holds). findingMode() runs a Bluefruit scan for 60 s and maps the strongest group RSSI to a pulse period of 2.5 s down to 0.25 s. hmac32() is a truncated HMAC so strangers' packets are ignored.

    Getting home

    "I'm home" texts, even with a dead phone

    Groups split at the end of the night: some take an Uber, some stay out. Firefly closes the loop so nobody has to wonder who made it back.

    1 · Triple-press

    When you're in the door, press Button 1 three times. Two short buzzes confirm. Friends whose bands are still in range get "Jordan is home" over LoRa straight away.

    2 · Your phone sends the text

    Out of range? The band holds the message. As soon as your phone is charging and back in Bluetooth range of your band, the Firefly app texts the people you picked, like "Jordan got home safe at 2:14 AM". Set the list once: Mom, a roommate, the group chat.

    3 · Everyone home?

    The responsible friend's app shows a "Getting home" list: home, on the way, or not heard from. If someone hasn't checked in by the time the session ends, the app nudges you to call them.

    Automatic option

    Anyone whose phone still has battery can turn on "Text when I arrive home". The app uses their saved home address and sends the same message when they get there, with no button needed. Home addresses stay on the phone and are never shared with the group.

    Connectivity

    How the band talks to the app

    Two radios, two jobs. Bluetooth links the responsible friend's band to their phone. LoRa links every band to every other band, so the group stays connected with no phone, Wi-Fi or cell signal.

    1 · Pair once

    Open the app and hold Button 1. The phone finds the band over Bluetooth and saves it, like pairing AirPods. After that it reconnects by itself whenever the band is in range.

    2 · Join the group

    Everyone holds Button 1 together for 5 seconds. The bands agree on a random group key over LoRa and buzz twice. From then on they only listen to each other.

    3 · Messages relay

    Jordan double-presses: his band sends a 12-byte encrypted packet over LoRa. Maya's band receives it and hands it to her phone over Bluetooth, and the app shows "Jordan: come find me · 29 m". "Ping their band" travels the same path in reverse.

    Group

    Each friend's band battery, whether their phone is alive, last check-in and approximate distance from the responsible friend. The person who needs attention floats to the top.

    Map

    Everyone is shown on a street map around the responsible friend. Only their phone has GPS, so friends are placed by band-to-band signal relative to it: an "about 15 m" estimate rather than a false pinpoint. If a second phone in the group is alive, two anchors give a real position.

    How to navigate back to a friend

    1. Open Group and tap Find › next to the friend, or tap their dot on the Map. An SOS or help alert has a Walk me there › button that does the same.
    2. Hold the phone flat. The big arrow points toward them and the number is the distance. The compass letters and the "near the bar" hint tell you where in the venue to head.
    3. Walk. The arrow and the signal bars update every second; when you enter Bluetooth range (25 m) your own band starts pulsing faster the closer you get.
    4. Tap Ping their band so their wrist buzzes three times and lights up; they stop and wait, and you can spot the glow in a dark room.
    5. Try it above: drag Maya (green) toward Jordan in the simulation and watch the arrow and distance change.

    Alerts

    Three tiers: check-in (quiet), come find me (notification), help and SOS (full-screen, loud, with one-tap call and a "walk me there" compass that follows signal strength). Cancels propagate everywhere within a second.

    Night session

    A session starts when the group taps bands together and ends automatically at a time the group picks (default 6 a.m.). When it ends, every band forgets the group key and the app deletes the night's location trail.

    Privacy & location

    Tracking that only the group can see, and only tonight

    No location on the band

    The band has no GPS and stores no coordinates. It knows only that another band with tonight's group key is near, and how strong its signal is. If you lose the band, there is nothing on it to read.

    Keys that expire

    Tapping bands together at the start of the night exchanges a random group key over NFC. Every radio message is encrypted with it, so a stranger with the same hardware sees only noise. The key is wiped when the session ends.

    Nothing leaves the group

    Band-to-band messages never touch a server. The responsible friend's phone keeps the night's data on the device and deletes the trail at session end. No account is needed to wear a band; only the app user signs in.

    Consent, visibly

    Joining a session is a physical act (the tap) with a confirmation buzz. Anyone can leave a session by holding both buttons for five seconds; the group gets a "left the group" check-in rather than a silent disappearance.

    Coarse by design

    Signal strength gives distance bands (within 5 m, 15 m, 50 m, in LoRa range), not a dot on a map. That is enough to find a friend in a club and too little to stalk someone across a city.

    Open questions

    Whether to offer an opt-in cloud relay for groups that split across venues (needs any phone to be alive); how to handle a group member who refuses a check-in; FCC Part 15 testing for the 915 MHz radio before any sale.

    How finding works, step by step

    • 0–25 m, same room: BLE signal strength between the two bands. The band pulses faster and lights more LEDs as you get warmer. No direction arrow; you sweep and follow the pulse, like a metal detector.
    • 25–300 m, next room or across the street: LoRa carries "come find me" and the reply, plus the sender's rough distance band. The app shows which direction the signal came from if the responsible friend moves.
    • Out of LoRa range: the band shows a slow red breathing LED so the wearer knows the group is gone. Any alive phone in the group can relay over cell if the opt-in relay is on.
